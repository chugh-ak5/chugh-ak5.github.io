02 Markets

Markets & Machines

Trading is a risk-allocation exercise that happens to be executed through charts. This page is how I run that exercise: what I read on a book, what I refuse to trade without, and the code that keeps me accountable to both.

01 Method

Read the book, then size the position.

My frames are deliberately narrow. I look for short-term structural momentum — where resting liquidity is thin, where participants are being forced to transact, where a level is defended or abandoned. Order-book imbalance, depth attrition and spread behaviour tell me more about the next hour than any indicator will.

Execution across FX, indices, commodities and crypto derivatives is a single discipline with different tick sizes. The instrument changes; the process shouldn't.

  • Frame Order-book imbalance surveillance — depth and aggressor flow at the touch, tracked continuously rather than sampled at a bar close.
  • Filter Volatility regime — position size scales down as realised volatility expands. Never the other way round.
  • Risk Hard drawdown ceilings — daily loss limits and trailing risk parameters are constraints, not suggestions. Capital preservation outranks any single thesis.
  • Review Daily attribution — P&L decomposed by instrument, session and thesis, with blotter reconciliation behind it. A strategy I can't explain next morning doesn't get repeated.

02 Hedging

Delta-neutral by construction, not by hope

On the desk I ran structured hedges against overnight gap risk: monitor live quote feeds and spread markups, then offset exposure with delta-neutral orders rather than simply reducing size and paying away the edge. The same instinct applies to crypto books, where the risk never sleeps because the venue doesn't.

  • Gap risk Overnight exposure hedged into the close using liquid offsets; the cost of the hedge is budgeted in advance, not negotiated at the open.
  • Spread integrity Quote-feed and markup monitoring so a widening spread is treated as a signal about liquidity, not as an invitation to trade.
  • Fill quality Execution reviewed against venue behaviour and slippage expectations; poor fills are a systems problem as often as a market one.

03 Stack

Machines that do the 3 a.m. work.

Everything repetitive on a desk is a bug waiting for a market event. My default is to encode it: pull the data, test the assumption, alert the human only when a threshold is breached.

LayerWhat it doesTooling
Ingest Market prices, quote feeds, order-book snapshots and platform throughput pulled on a schedule or streamed continuously. Python · pandas · NumPy · venue REST & WebSocket APIs
Store Time-series history for liquidity metrics, latency and exposure — queryable months later when a strategy needs a post-mortem. SQL · InfluxDB
Model Position and exposure maths, risk-adjusted performance (Sharpe, win rate), stress and scenario passes, backtests with realistic costs. Python · SQL · advanced Excel for desk-facing work
Observe Dashboards for trade flow, liquidity depth, quote latency; threshold alarms routed to the channel a human actually reads. Grafana · InfluxDB · scheduled monitors & alert dispatch
Reconcile Daily blotter, position and payment reconciliation — automated, exception-first, audit-ready. Python · SQL · cloud-hosted jobs

04 AI & automation

AI as an operator, not an oracle.

Language models are genuinely good at the unglamorous middle of a trading workflow: parsing a filing, summarising a research note, drafting the reconciliation report, writing the glue code that connects two APIs. They are not a source of edge, and anyone treating a model's confidence as a probability has confused fluency with calibration.

So I use them where being approximately right is cheap and being slow is expensive — and I keep deterministic code on the paths where a wrong answer costs money.

  • Research Document triage, filings and paper summaries, and code translation for unfamiliar APIs.
  • Engineering Agentic workflows that write, test and iterate on the plumbing code around a strategy — monitored, version-controlled, and never trusted with order routing.
  • Guardrails Every automated action has a dry-run, a cost ceiling and a reversal path. If I can't undo it, it doesn't run unattended.
  • Study Currently completing Agentic AI and Applications at IIT Madras to keep the engineering honest.

05 Reading list

The ideas I keep coming back to are boring ones: risk of ruin before expectancy, transaction costs as a first-class input, and the difference between a strategy that worked and a strategy that works.

  • Position sizingKelly under uncertainty — and why fractional is almost always the right answer.
  • MicrostructureOrder-book mechanics, adverse selection, and why the spread is information.
  • Backtest hygieneLook-ahead bias, survivorship, cost realism, and the discipline of an untouched out-of-sample window.
