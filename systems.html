04 Systems

Linux, cloud
and the discipline
between them.

I run things. Trading infrastructure, research jobs, monitors, backups — mostly headless, mostly unattended, on machines I have to be able to reason about at 4 a.m. That's the whole reason security interests me: not as a career in tick-boxing, but as the difference between a system that survives contact with the internet and one that becomes someone else's.

01 Linux

The terminal is the interface.

Arch on the workstation, Debian/Ubuntu on servers, systemd for anything that needs to stay up. I'd rather understand ten moving parts than trust a control panel with forty of them — and when something breaks at an awkward hour, the machine I can debug fastest is the one I chose on purpose.

My daily work is text: SSH, tmux, git, logs, dashboards over a terminal. It makes remote work on a French desk and a Chennai desk indistinguishable.

  • WorkstationArch Linux, rolling, deliberately minimal — no GUI browser in the loop for anything I automate.
  • ServersDebian/Ubuntu LTS, unattended-upgrades, systemd units with restart policies, journald for anything that failed at 03:00.
  • Process disciplineLong jobs get scheduled, logged and alerted — not left in a terminal hoping I remember.
  • BackupsSnapshot before risky operations, off-box copies, and a restore path that has actually been tested.

02 Security

Hardening is just operations done properly.

The failures that cost money are rarely exotic. They're a port nobody needed, a key reused across hosts, a token committed to a repository, or a service running as root because it was faster that afternoon.

LayerPracticeWhy
Access Key-only SSH, one key per machine, no password authentication, non-root by default A shared key is a shared compromise; per-host keys mean a leak has a blast radius of one.
Exposure Nothing published unless it must be; private services reached over a mesh VPN or an authenticated tunnel rather than an open port The best-defended service is the one with no listening socket on the public internet.
Privilege Least privilege for every process, explicit sudo, no root-owned application code Containment is a design choice made before the incident, not during it.
Secrets Environment files outside version control, rotated on suspicion, never echoed into logs Credential leakage is the most common self-inflicted breach there is.
Visibility Alerting on failed logins, disk pressure, service restarts and unexpected egress You cannot respond to what you didn't notice — and noticing is a cron job.
Recoverability Tested restore paths, immutable-ish backups, runbooks for the two failures that actually happen Availability is a security property too; downtime is how a small problem becomes an incident.

Tools and practice

Kali tooling and a pentest command reference live in my toolbox for lab ranges and self-audits — reconnaissance, service enumeration, and the unglamorous work of knowing what my own machines expose. The value is in the audit habit: scan your own perimeter on a schedule, and treat every surprise as a task, not a scare.

  • ReconEnumerate your own surface before someone else does — ports, certificates, DNS records, forgotten subdomains.
  • Lab workDeliberately vulnerable ranges and CTF-style exercises for practice, kept strictly off production networks.
  • ScriptingPython and shell for anything repetitive: log triage, integrity checks, configuration drift.

03 Cloud

Infrastructure at the price of discipline.

I have firm opinions about cloud cost, because I've paid for compute I didn't need. A trading monitor, a research job and a dashboard do not require a managed platform — they require a small Linux box, good process hygiene and honest alerting.

Most of what I run today sits on always-free capacity: an ARM instance for scheduled jobs and services, with storage and egress watched deliberately, because "free tier" is an accounting limit, not a personality trait of the provider.

  • PlatformsAWS and GCP for managed services and data work; Oracle Cloud always-free ARM for long-running jobs; Cloudflare in front for DNS, TLS and edge delivery.
  • Sizing disciplineRight-size, then resist the upgrade. Swap before you blame RAM; measure before you resize.
  • Cost hygieneBudget alerts set the day a tenancy goes pay-as-you-go, and a monthly review of anything that grew a line item.
  • NetworkingMesh VPN for private access between machines, tunnels instead of open ports, DNS over a provider that gives TLS for free.

04 Operating rules

Automate it, alert on it, or accept that you own it — those are the three options, and the third one has a way of choosing itself at the worst moment.
  • Rule 01A service that isn't monitored isn't running; it's just not failing loudly yet.
  • Rule 02Every scheduled job writes a log and a heartbeat, so silence is distinguishable from death.
  • Rule 03Test the restore, not the backup. An unverified archive is a rumour.
  • Rule 04Cost is a design constraint. If a system needs a bigger instance every quarter, the architecture is the problem.
