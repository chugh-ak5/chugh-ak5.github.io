01 The approach

Maya and Shiv

Two ideas from my own tradition that explain, better than any education phrase, what I think a school is for. One is the world as it appears. The other is the attention that sees through it. A child needs to be given both.

01 The two ideas

Maya — the world as it appears

Maya is form, appearance, imagination: the shape a thing takes, the story we tell about it, the picture a child builds in their head before the words arrive. It is also the reminder that appearance is not the whole of the thing — that the surface answers first and often answers wrongly.

In a classroom this is the world of activity, theatre, music, experiment and expression. It is where a child learns that they can make something that did not exist before.

Shiv — the attention that watches

Shiv is awareness: the part of a person that can stand back from what is happening and see it clearly. Stillness, discernment, the choice that follows.

In a classroom this is the harder half — teaching a child to notice their own thinking, to hold a question open, to disagree without heat, and to look at another person's reality without flinching. It is what turns information into judgement.

A classroom that only rewards the correct answer trains memory. A classroom that also trains attention produces something more durable.
Why the distinction matters

02 In practice

What that looks like on a Monday morning.

  • Curriculum Activity before abstraction. Concepts delivered through work the child can do, see and argue about — reading, drama, discussion, project work — mapped honestly onto CBSE, ICSE or IB requirements rather than smuggled in beside them.
  • Assessment Feedback that describes. Feedback should tell a child what their thinking did, not only whether it was right. Marks are recorded; so is what they reveal.
  • Teachers Development over inspection. Lesson observations exist to improve teaching, not to grade it. Workshops, peer practice and coaching for teachers are a timetable commitment, not an annual event.
  • Counselling The child first, the record second. Guidance starts with listening — to the student, and to the family — before it becomes a plan, a stream choice or an application strategy.
  • Families Parents inside the conversation. Regular, plain communication through PTAs and advisory sessions, so a difficulty is met early instead of escalating quietly.
  • The wider world Exchange and service, not extra-curricular garnish. Intercultural programmes, Model UN, community service and the arts are where attention to other people's realities is actually learned.
